1.1 What is Finance?

Concept Explanation

Finance is the science of managing money. At its core, finance deals with three fundamental questions:

  1. Where should we invest money? (Investment Decision)
  2. Where should we raise money from? (Financing Decision)
  3. How much should we return to investors? (Dividend Decision)

Finance Categories

1. Corporate Finance: How businesses manage their money, make investment decisions, and fund operations.

2. Investment Finance: How investors allocate money across different assets (stocks, bonds, real estate) to maximize returns.

3. Personal Finance: How individuals manage income, expenses, savings, and investments.

Every business decision has a financial implication. Finance professionals help answer critical questions using data, analysis, and financial models—exactly the skills you've built through your AI automation and analytics work at SoulSage!
Indian Company Context: Tata Motors

Situation: Tata Motors wanted to acquire Jaguar Land Rover (JLR) from Ford for $2.3 billion in 2008.

Finance Questions:

  • Investment Decision: Is JLR worth $2.3 billion? Will it generate enough cash flows to justify the price?
  • Financing Decision: Should Tata raise money through bank loans, bonds, or by selling equity?
  • Dividend Decision: Should they cut dividends to existing shareholders to fund the acquisition?

Outcome: Tata used a mix of debt and internal cash. The acquisition initially struggled but eventually became highly profitable, validating the investment decision.

2.1 Introduction to Financial Statements

Financial statements are formal records of a company's financial activities. They provide a standardized way to communicate financial information to stakeholders.

The Three Core Financial Statements:

1. Income Statement (Profit & Loss Statement)

  • Shows: Revenue, expenses, and profit over a period
  • Time Period: Quarter or Year (e.g., FY 2022-23)
  • Answers: "How much profit did we make?"

2. Balance Sheet

  • Shows: Assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time
  • Time Point: As of March 31, 2023
  • Answers: "What does the company own and owe?"

3. Cash Flow Statement

  • Shows: Cash inflows and outflows over a period
  • Time Period: Quarter or Year
  • Answers: "How much actual cash did we generate?"
Each statement tells a different part of the story: Income Statement = Performance (like a report card), Balance Sheet = Position (like a snapshot of wealth), Cash Flow Statement = Reality check (profit does not equal cash!)
Why Profit Does Not Equal Cash

Scenario: Your friend has a small business selling laptops.

Month 1:

  • Sold 10 laptops at ₹50,000 each = ₹5,00,000 revenue
  • Cost per laptop = ₹40,000, so cost = ₹4,00,000
  • Profit = ₹1,00,000 (looks great!)

But...

  • Customers paid ₹0 in cash (all bought on credit—"pay in 60 days")
  • Your friend paid ₹4,00,000 cash to supplier immediately
  • Cash Flow = -₹4,00,000 (cash burn!)

Result: Income Statement shows ₹1,00,000 profit. Cash Flow Statement shows -₹4,00,000 cash burn. Without cash, the business could collapse despite being "profitable"!

This is why analysts look at ALL three statements.

2.2 Income Statement (Profit & Loss Statement)

The Income Statement shows a company's financial performance over a period (quarter/year). It follows a simple logic: Revenue - Expenses = Profit

Revenue from Operations (Sales) (-) Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) = Gross Profit (-) Operating Expenses - Employee costs - Administrative expenses - Selling & marketing expenses = Operating Profit (EBIT) (-) Interest Expense (-) Depreciation & Amortization = Profit Before Tax (PBT) (-) Tax Expense = Profit After Tax (PAT / Net Profit)

Key Terminology:

Revenue/Sales/Turnover: Money earned from selling products/services ("Top line")

Cost of Goods Sold (COGS): Direct costs of producing goods sold (raw materials, direct labor)

Gross Profit: Revenue - COGS. Indicates pricing power and production efficiency.

Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100

Operating Expenses (OPEX): Costs of running the business (salaries, rent, marketing, R&D)

EBITDA: Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, Amortization. Measures operating performance.

EBIT: Earnings Before Interest and Tax. Operating profit.

PAT (Profit After Tax) / Net Profit: Final profit after all expenses and taxes. "Bottom line" that belongs to shareholders.

2.3 Balance Sheet

The Balance Sheet is a snapshot of a company's financial position at a specific point in time.

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity This equation ALWAYS balances (hence "Balance Sheet").

Three Main Components:

1. ASSETS (What the company owns)

Current Assets (can be converted to cash within 1 year):

  • Cash and Bank Balances
  • Accounts Receivable (money customers owe)
  • Inventory (goods in stock)
  • Short-term Investments

Non-Current Assets (long-term):

  • Property, Plant & Equipment (PP&E) - factories, machines
  • Intangible Assets (patents, brands, goodwill)
  • Long-term Investments

2. LIABILITIES (What the company owes)

Current Liabilities (due within 1 year):

  • Accounts Payable (money owed to suppliers)
  • Short-term Debt
  • Provisions and accruals

Non-Current Liabilities (long-term):

  • Long-term Debt (bonds, loans)
  • Deferred Tax Liabilities
  • Long-term Provisions

3. SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY (Owners' claim)

  • Share Capital (money from issuing shares)
  • Reserves & Surplus (retained earnings)
  • Total Equity = Assets - Liabilities

3.1 Introduction to Ratio Analysis

Financial ratios are mathematical relationships between numbers from financial statements. They help us assess performance, compare over time, compare across companies, and identify red flags.

Category Purpose Key Question
Profitability Measure ability to generate profit How profitable is the company?
Liquidity Measure ability to meet short-term obligations Can it pay bills due within a year?
Efficiency Measure how well assets are used How efficiently does it operate?
Leverage Measure financial risk and debt levels How much debt does it have?
Valuation Measure market value relative to fundamentals Is the stock overvalued or undervalued?
Never use ratios in isolation. Compare to the company's past performance, compare to competitors, and understand industry context. High debt is risky for a startup but normal for a bank!

3.2 Key Financial Ratios Reference

Profitability Ratios

1. Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100 Interpretation: High GPM (50%+) = Strong pricing power 2. Operating Profit Margin = (EBIT / Revenue) × 100 Interpretation: High OPM (20%+) = Efficient operations 3. Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100 Interpretation: High NPM (15%+) = Very profitable 4. Return on Assets (ROA) = (Net Profit / Total Assets) × 100 Interpretation: High ROA (>15%) = Asset-light business 5. Return on Equity (ROE) = (Net Profit / Equity) × 100 ⭐ MOST IMPORTANT FOR INVESTORS Interpretation: ROE >15% = Excellent, 10-15% = Good, <10% = Concerning 6. Return on Capital Employed = (EBIT / Capital Employed) × 100 Where Capital Employed = Total Assets - Current Liabilities

Liquidity Ratios

1. Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities Interpretation: >2 = Very safe, 1.5-2 = Comfortable, <1 = Warning 2. Quick Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities Interpretation: >1 = Good, 0.5-1 = Adequate, <0.5 = Liquidity stress 3. Cash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents) / Current Liabilities Interpretation: >0.5 = Very strong, 0.2-0.5 = Adequate

Efficiency Ratios

1. Asset Turnover = Revenue / Total Assets Interpretation: Higher = More efficient asset utilization 2. Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory Interpretation: Higher = Faster inventory movement (good for perishables) 3. Receivables Turnover = Revenue / Accounts Receivable Interpretation: Higher = Faster collection from customers 4. Payables Turnover = COGS / Accounts Payable Interpretation: Lower = Better cash management (delaying payments)

Leverage Ratios

1. Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt / Total Equity Interpretation: <0.5 = Conservative, 0.5-1 = Moderate, >2 = High leverage 2. Debt-to-Assets = Total Debt / Total Assets Interpretation: <0.3 = Low debt, >0.6 = High debt 3. Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense Interpretation: >3 = Safe, 1-2 = Risky, <1 = Cannot cover interest 4. Debt Service Coverage = Operating Cash Flow / Total Debt Service Interpretation: >1.5 = Comfortable debt servicing ability

Valuation Ratios (Market-based)

1. Price-to-Earnings (P/E) = Market Price per Share / Earnings per Share Interpretation: Low P/E = Potentially undervalued, High P/E = Growth expectations 2. Price-to-Book (P/B) = Market Price per Share / Book Value per Share Interpretation: P/B < 1 = Trading below book value (potential value) 3. Price-to-Sales (P/S) = Market Cap / Revenue Interpretation: Used for loss-making companies 4. Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend per Share / Market Price × 100 Interpretation: Higher = Better income for investors 5. Earnings per Share (EPS) = Net Profit / Number of Shares Fundamental measure of per-share profitability

Interview Speak for Nakul: "What's the difference between ROE and ROA?"
"ROA measures how efficiently a company uses its total assets to generate profit—it's calculated as net profit divided by total assets. ROE measures the return on shareholders' equity specifically—net profit divided by equity. The key difference is that ROE reflects the impact of leverage. A company can improve ROE by taking on debt, which isn't reflected in ROA. For equity investors, ROE is typically more important because it shows returns on their specific investment. However, very high ROE driven by excessive leverage can be risky. That's why I'd look at both: ROE for understanding shareholder returns, and ROA to assess operational efficiency independently of capital structure. For example, HDFC Bank has ROE of 18% but ROA of only 2%—this reflects banking's leveraged business model where deposits (liabilities) fund loans (assets)."

4.1 Time Value of Money

The fundamental principle of finance: A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow.

Why?

  • Opportunity Cost: Money today can be invested to earn returns
  • Inflation: Money loses purchasing power over time
  • Risk: Future cash is uncertain; present cash is certain

Example: Would you prefer ₹10,000 today or ₹10,000 in 5 years?

Answer: ₹10,000 today! You can invest it at 8% and have ₹14,693 in 5 years.

Future Value (FV) = Present Value (PV) × (1 + r)^n Where: - PV = Present Value (money today) - FV = Future Value (money in the future) - r = Interest rate / discount rate - n = Number of periods (years) Present Value (PV) = FV / (1 + r)^n
Calculation Example

Question: You will receive ₹1,00,000 in 3 years. If the discount rate is 10%, what is the Present Value?

PV = FV / (1 + r)^n PV = 1,00,000 / (1 + 0.10)^3 PV = 1,00,000 / 1.331 PV = ₹75,131 Interpretation: ₹1,00,000 received in 3 years is worth only ₹75,131 today.
Discounting is the reverse of compounding. When we "discount" future cash flows, we're calculating their value in today's terms. This is the foundation of valuation!

4.2 DCF Valuation Method

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) is the gold standard of valuation. It values a company based on its future cash flows, discounted to present value.

DCF Formula:

Company Value = Σ (Future Cash Flows / (1 + WACC)^n) Steps: 1. Forecast Free Cash Flows for 5-10 years 2. Calculate Terminal Value (value beyond forecast period) 3. Discount all cash flows to present using WACC 4. Sum up to get Enterprise Value 5. Subtract Net Debt to get Equity Value 6. Divide by shares outstanding to get Fair Value per share

WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

WACC is the discount rate used in DCF. It represents the company's average cost of raising capital.

WACC = (E/V × Cost of Equity) + (D/V × Cost of Debt × (1 - Tax Rate)) Where: - E = Market value of Equity - D = Market value of Debt - V = E + D (Total Value) - Cost of Equity = Risk-free rate + Beta × Market risk premium - Cost of Debt = Interest rate on borrowing Example: Infosys WACC ≈ 11-12% HDFC Bank WACC ≈ 10-11% Reliance WACC ≈ 12-13%
Interview Speak for Nakul: "Walk me through a DCF valuation."
"A DCF valuation determines a company's intrinsic value by discounting its future free cash flows to present value. First, I'd forecast free cash flows for typically 5-10 years, using historical growth rates, industry trends, and management guidance. Free cash flow equals operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Next, I'd calculate terminal value using either the perpetuity growth method or exit multiple method, representing value beyond the forecast period. Then, I'd determine the appropriate discount rate—typically WACC, which reflects the company's cost of capital. I'd discount all future cash flows and terminal value back to present using WACC. This gives enterprise value. I'd subtract net debt to get equity value, then divide by shares outstanding for per-share value. For sensitivity analysis, I'd flex key assumptions like growth rate and WACC to understand valuation range. In my work at SoulSage evaluating campaign ROI, I used similar present value concepts to assess whether projected returns justified upfront investments—essentially a mini-DCF framework."

6.2 Industry Analysis Framework

Porter's Five Forces Analysis

1. Threat of New Entrants

  • How easy is it for new competitors to enter?
  • Barriers: Capital requirements, regulations, brand loyalty, economies of scale
  • Example: Banking (high barriers—RBI license, capital requirements) vs E-commerce (low barriers)

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers

  • Can suppliers dictate terms?
  • Example: Apple has low supplier power (many component manufacturers). Diamond industry has high supplier power (few mine owners)

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers

  • Can customers negotiate prices?
  • Example: Enterprise software (low buyer power—switching costs high) vs Commodities (high buyer power—many alternatives)

4. Threat of Substitutes

  • Are there alternative products/services?
  • Example: Traditional taxis faced substitution from Ola/Uber. Movie theaters face substitution from OTT platforms

5. Competitive Rivalry

  • How intense is competition?
  • Example: Telecom (intense—Jio, Airtel, Vi) vs Credit rating agencies (low—only CRISIL, ICRA, CARE)

Optimal Capital Structure

The "right" mix of debt and equity that minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value.

  • Too much debt: Financial distress risk, bankruptcy potential
  • Too little debt: Missing tax benefits, not optimizing cost of capital

Industry Benchmarks:

The tax shield on debt makes it cheaper than equity. Interest expense reduces taxable income, so effective cost of debt = Interest Rate × (1 - Tax Rate). If interest is 10% and tax rate is 25%, effective cost = 10% × 0.75 = 7.5%.

7.2 Capital Budgeting: NPV & IRR

Capital budgeting helps companies decide which long-term investments to pursue.

Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV = Σ (Cash Flow_t / (1 + r)^t) - Initial Investment Decision Rule: - NPV > 0 → Accept project (creates value) - NPV < 0 → Reject project (destroys value) - NPV = 0 → Indifferent

Example:

Reliance considering investing ₹100 crore in a new refinery. Expected cash flows:

  • Year 1: ₹30 crore
  • Year 2: ₹40 crore
  • Year 3: ₹50 crore
  • Discount rate (WACC): 12%
NPV = (30/1.12^1) + (40/1.12^2) + (50/1.12^3) - 100 = 26.79 + 31.89 + 35.59 - 100 = ₹94.27 - ₹100 = -₹5.73 crore Decision: REJECT (NPV is negative—project destroys value)

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

IRR = Discount rate at which NPV = 0 Decision Rule: - IRR > WACC → Accept project - IRR < WACC → Reject project IRR represents the project's expected return.

Example continued:

If IRR is calculated as 10.5%, and WACC is 12%, then IRR < WACC → REJECT

Real Example: Amazon India Expansion Decision

Scenario: Amazon evaluating ₹5,000 crore investment in India logistics infrastructure

Capital Budgeting Analysis:

  • Initial Investment: ₹5,000 crore (warehouses, delivery network)
  • Expected incremental cash flows: ₹800 crore/year for 10 years
  • Terminal value: ₹3,000 crore (asset sale value after 10 years)
  • WACC: 14%

NPV Calculation:

PV of annual cash flows + PV of terminal value - Initial investment

= ₹4,171 + ₹809 - ₹5,000 = -₹20 crore

Decision: MARGINAL. NPV slightly negative, but strategic value (market share, customer acquisition, competitive positioning) might justify investment despite negative financial NPV.

8.2 50+ Technical Finance Interview Q&A

Q1: What are the three financial statements?
The three financial statements are the Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow Statement. The Income Statement shows profitability over a period. The Balance Sheet shows financial position at a point in time. The Cash Flow Statement shows actual cash movements. They're interconnected: net profit flows from Income Statement to retained earnings on the Balance Sheet and serves as the starting point for operating cash flow.
Q2: Walk me through a DCF valuation.
A DCF values a company by discounting future free cash flows to present value. I'd forecast FCF for 5-10 years based on revenue growth and margin assumptions, calculate terminal value using perpetuity growth or exit multiple, discount everything using WACC, sum to get enterprise value, subtract net debt for equity value, and divide by shares for per-share value. Key sensitivities are growth rate, margins, and discount rate.
Q3: What's the difference between ROE and ROA?
ROA measures profit relative to total assets—operational efficiency independent of financing. ROE measures profit relative to shareholders' equity—returns to equity holders. The difference reflects leverage. A company can boost ROE through debt, but this increases financial risk. For example, HDFC Bank has 18% ROE but only 2% ROA, reflecting banking's leveraged model. I'd look at both to understand operational efficiency (ROA) and shareholder returns (ROE) separately.
Q4: How do you value a bank?
Banks are typically valued using P/B and P/E multiples rather than DCF because their balance sheets are their business—assets are loans, liabilities are deposits. I'd focus on P/B ratio comparing market cap to book value, adjusted for asset quality. Key metrics include ROE (profitability), net NPA ratio (asset quality), and capital adequacy ratio (buffer against losses). For example, HDFC Bank trades at 3x P/B due to superior ROE and asset quality. I'd also look at loan growth, deposit franchise strength, and cost-to-income ratio for operational efficiency.
Q5: Why might a company have negative cash flow despite being profitable?
Several reasons: high capital expenditures (investing in growth), building up inventory or receivables (working capital increase), or using accrual accounting where revenue is recognized before cash collection. For example, Zomato might show profit from food delivery while burning cash on expansion and customer acquisition. I'd check if negative cash flow is from operations (concerning) or investing activities (potentially strategic growth investment). Sustainable businesses must eventually convert profit to positive operating cash flow.
Q6: What's EBITDA and why do we use it?
EBITDA is Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortization. It measures operating performance before financing decisions (interest), tax jurisdictions (tax), and accounting policies (depreciation). It's useful for comparing companies across different capital structures and tax regimes. However, it ignores capex requirements and working capital needs, so I'd never use it alone—always complement with free cash flow analysis. It's particularly useful in capital-intensive industries for comparing operating efficiency.
Q7: How would you analyze a company if you only had 30 minutes?
I'd prioritize: First, understand the business model and revenue drivers (5 mins—read latest annual report intro). Second, analyze financial health—look at last 3 years' revenue growth, margins, ROE, and debt-to-equity (10 mins—screener.in for quick ratios). Third, check recent news and management commentary for red flags or catalysts (5 mins). Fourth, quick valuation check using P/E vs peers and historical average (5 mins). Finally, form a preliminary view on investment thesis and key risks (5 mins). This gives a 80% understanding efficiently, which I'd deep-dive later if promising.

Finance Glossary: 100+ Essential Terms

Your comprehensive reference for finance terminology. Master these terms to speak the language of finance fluently.

A - D

Accrual Accounting

Accounting method where revenues and expenses are recorded when earned/incurred, not when cash is exchanged. Matches revenues with related expenses.

Asset

Resource owned by a company that has economic value. Examples: cash, inventory, property, equipment. Classified as current (liquid within 1 year) or non-current (long-term).

Balance Sheet

Financial statement showing company's assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time. Formula: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

Beta

Measure of a stock's volatility relative to the overall market. Beta=1 means stock moves with market; Beta>1 more volatile; Beta<1 less volatile.

Book Value

Net asset value of a company (Total Assets - Total Liabilities). Book Value per Share = Book Value / Number of Shares Outstanding.

Capital Expenditure (Capex)

Funds used to acquire or upgrade physical assets like property, plants, or equipment. Long-term investment in business infrastructure.

Cash Flow Statement

Financial statement showing cash inflows and outflows from operating, investing, and financing activities over a period.

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

Average annual growth rate over a specified period longer than one year. Formula: (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/years) - 1

Current Ratio

Liquidity ratio measuring ability to pay short-term obligations. Formula: Current Assets / Current Liabilities. Ratio > 1.5 generally considered healthy.

DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)

Valuation method that estimates company value by discounting projected future cash flows to present value using WACC.

Debt-to-Equity Ratio

Leverage ratio showing proportion of debt to equity financing. Formula: Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity. Higher ratio = more leverage = more risk.

Depreciation

Non-cash expense allocating cost of tangible assets over their useful life. Reduces asset value on balance sheet and profit on income statement.

Dividend

Portion of company profits distributed to shareholders. Can be cash or stock dividends. Indicates financial health and management confidence.

Dividend Yield

Annual dividend per share divided by stock price, expressed as percentage. Indicates income return on investment. Formula: (Annual Dividend / Price) × 100

E - N

EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Tax)

Operating profit measure showing earnings from core operations before financing costs and taxes. Also called Operating Profit.

EBITDA

Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortization. Measures operating performance excluding non-cash expenses and financing decisions.

Enterprise Value (EV)

Total value of a company. Formula: Market Cap + Total Debt - Cash. Used in valuation multiples like EV/EBITDA.

EPS (Earnings Per Share)

Company's net profit divided by number of outstanding shares. Key metric for per-share profitability. Formula: Net Profit / Shares Outstanding.

Equity

Ownership interest in a company (stocks/shares). Also refers to Shareholders' Equity = Assets - Liabilities on balance sheet.

Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Cash generated after accounting for capital expenditures. Formula: Operating Cash Flow - Capex. Indicates cash available for distribution to shareholders.

Gross Profit Margin

Profitability ratio showing percentage of revenue retained after cost of goods sold. Formula: (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100. Indicates pricing power.

Income Statement

Financial statement showing revenues, expenses, and profit over a period. Also called Profit & Loss (P&L) Statement. Shows company's financial performance.

IPO (Initial Public Offering)

Process where a private company offers shares to the public for first time, becoming publicly traded on stock exchanges.

IRR (Internal Rate of Return)

Discount rate at which NPV of an investment equals zero. Represents the expected annual return of a project. Used in capital budgeting decisions.

Liability

Financial obligation or debt owed by a company. Examples: loans, accounts payable, bonds. Classified as current (due within 1 year) or long-term.

Liquidity

Ability to convert assets to cash quickly or meet short-term obligations. Cash is most liquid asset; real estate is illiquid.

Market Capitalization

Total market value of a company's outstanding shares. Formula: Share Price × Number of Shares Outstanding. Categorized as large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap.

Mutual Fund

Investment vehicle pooling money from many investors to invest in diversified portfolio of stocks, bonds, or other securities. Professionally managed.

Net Profit Margin

Profitability ratio showing percentage of revenue retained as profit after all expenses. Formula: (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100. Indicates overall efficiency.

NPV (Net Present Value)

Sum of present values of future cash flows minus initial investment. Positive NPV means project creates value. Key capital budgeting metric.

P - Z

P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings)

Valuation multiple showing how much investors pay per rupee of earnings. Formula: Stock Price / EPS. Higher P/E suggests growth expectations or overvaluation.

P/B Ratio (Price-to-Book)

Valuation multiple comparing market value to book value. Formula: Stock Price / Book Value per Share. P/B < 1 may indicate undervaluation.

ROA (Return on Assets)

Profitability ratio measuring how efficiently company uses assets to generate profit. Formula: (Net Profit / Total Assets) × 100. Higher = better asset utilization.

ROE (Return on Equity)

Most important profitability ratio for investors. Measures return generated on shareholders' equity. Formula: (Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity) × 100. ROE >15% is excellent.

Revenue

Total income from sales of goods/services. "Top line" of income statement. Also called Sales or Turnover. Does not include expenses.

SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)

Regulatory body overseeing securities markets in India. Protects investors, regulates stock exchanges, and enforces securities laws.

Sensex

Stock market index of 30 largest and most actively traded stocks on Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). Benchmark for Indian equity market performance.

Valuation

Process of determining the intrinsic or fair value of a company or asset. Methods include DCF, comparable company analysis, precedent transactions.

WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

Average rate company pays to finance assets, weighted by proportion of debt and equity. Used as discount rate in DCF valuation.

Working Capital

Measure of company's short-term financial health. Formula: Current Assets - Current Liabilities. Positive working capital indicates ability to cover short-term obligations.

Yield

Income return on investment, expressed as percentage. For stocks: dividend yield. For bonds: interest yield. Indicates income generation potential.
