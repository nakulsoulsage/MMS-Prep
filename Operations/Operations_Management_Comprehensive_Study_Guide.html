1. Introduction to Operations Management

Reference: Lecture 1 - OM MMS

What is Operations Management?

Operations Management (OM)

OM is the strategic management of processes and resources that transform inputs into desired outputs. It optimizes efficiency, quality, and customer satisfaction across all business functions.

Operations Management is at the heart of every successful business. Whether you're manufacturing automobiles, delivering food through apps, or providing banking services, OM principles govern how effectively and efficiently these activities are performed.

Core Functions of OM

  • Transformation Process: Converting inputs (materials, labor, capital, information) into outputs (products/services)
  • Resource Optimization: Maximizing the utilization of available resources
  • Quality Assurance: Ensuring products/services meet specified standards
  • Process Design: Creating efficient workflows and procedures
  • Capacity Planning: Balancing supply capabilities with demand requirements

Objectives of Operations Management

1. Efficiency

Maximize resource utilization and minimize costs to achieve operational excellence. This involves reducing waste, optimizing processes, and improving productivity.

2. Quality

Deliver products and services that meet or exceed customer expectations. Quality encompasses reliability, durability, performance, and consistency.

3. Effectiveness

Producing the right kind of goods and services that satisfy customer needs. Being effective means doing the right things, not just doing things right.

4. Lead Time Reduction

Minimizing throughput time - the time that elapses in the conversion process - by reducing delays, waiting time, and idle time throughout the system.

5. Capacity Utilization

Maximizing utilization of manpower, machines, and other resources to ensure no capacity sits idle unnecessarily while maintaining flexibility.

6. Cost Minimization

Minimizing the cost of producing goods or rendering services without compromising on quality or customer satisfaction.

Scope of Operations Management

OM covers a comprehensive range of activities that span the entire production and delivery cycle:

Strategic Functions
  • Plant Location: Selecting optimal facility sites
  • Facilities Layout: Arranging equipment and workflows
  • Product Design: Creating products that meet market needs
  • Process Design: Designing efficient production methods
  • Capacity Planning: Determining production capabilities
Operational Functions
  • Production Planning & Control: Scheduling and monitoring production
  • Quality Control: Ensuring product/service quality
  • Materials Management: Managing inventory and supplies
  • Maintenance Management: Keeping equipment operational
  • Material Handling: Moving materials efficiently

Key Decision Areas in OM

  1. Product Selection: Determining what products/services to offer
  2. Product Design: Designing products for manufacturability and customer appeal
  3. Selection of Equipment and Processes: Choosing appropriate technology
  4. Job Design: Organizing work tasks and responsibilities
  5. Facility Layout: Arranging physical resources optimally
  6. Inventory Control: Managing stock levels
  7. Production Control: Coordinating production activities
  8. Quality Control: Maintaining quality standards
  9. Maintenance Management: Ensuring equipment reliability
  10. Cost Control and Improvement: Continuous cost optimization

Applications in Product and Service Industries

Product Industry (Manufacturing)
  • OM ensures efficient production processes
  • Quality control at every production stage
  • Timely delivery of finished goods

2. Plant Location

Reference: Lecture 2 - OM

Definition and Importance

Plant Location

Selection of a specific site for the establishment of the physical unit of production process. It is a strategic, long-term decision that significantly impacts an organization's costs, efficiency, and competitive position.

Plant location is one of the most critical strategic decisions as it involves substantial capital investment and has long-term implications. A wrong location decision can result in ongoing inefficiencies, higher costs, and competitive disadvantage that may be difficult to reverse.

Need for Plant Location Decisions

For New Factory
  • First-time establishment of manufacturing facility
  • Strategic expansion into new markets
  • Diversification into new product lines
  • New business venture or startup
For Existing Factory (Relocation/Expansion)
  • Change in availability of resources (labor, raw materials)
  • Shift of demand to different geographical areas
  • Identification of new target markets
  • Adoption of new technology requiring different facilities
  • Socio-political changes affecting current location
  • Lease expiration or infrastructure inadequacy
  • Environmental regulations at current location

Levels of Location Decisions

Level 1: Country Selection

Factors to consider when selecting a country for operations:

  • Political Environment: Political risks, government stability, rules, regulations, and attitudes toward foreign investment
  • Cultural and Economic Factors: Business culture, economic stability, growth potential, GDP trends
  • Labor Market: Availability of skilled labor, productivity levels, wage rates, union strength
  • Infrastructure: Availability of supplies, communication networks, energy reliability
  • Financial Considerations: Exchange rates, currency risks, tax structures, repatriation policies
  • Legal Framework: Intellectual property protection, contract enforcement, business laws

Level 2: Region/Community Selection

Factors for selecting a specific region within a country:

  • Labor Considerations: Local labor availability, costs, skill levels, union attitudes
  • Utilities: Costs and availability of electricity, water, gas, telecommunications
  • Regulatory Environment: Environmental regulations, building codes, zoning laws
  • Government Support: Tax incentives, grants, subsidies, fiscal policies
  • Proximity Factors: Distance to raw materials, suppliers, and customer markets
  • Quality of Life: Housing, schools, healthcare, recreation facilities

Level 3: Site Selection

Specific site-level considerations:

  • Site Characteristics: Size, cost, topography, soil conditions, room for expansion
  • Transportation: Access to air, rail, highway, and water transport systems
  • Zoning: Industrial zoning restrictions and land use regulations
  • Environmental Impact: Environmental assessment requirements, potential issues
  • Existing Infrastructure: Buildings, roads, utilities already on site

Location Analysis Methods

1. Factor Rating Method (Qualitative)

A systematic approach to evaluate multiple location alternatives against important factors:

Identify Important Factors List all factors that are important for the location decision (labor, transportation, utilities, etc.)
Assign Weights Rate each factor by its relative importance (weights should sum to 100% or 1.0)
Score Each Location Assign scores (typically 1-10 or 1-100) to each location for each factor
Calculate Weighted Scores Multiply factor weight by location score for each factor
Select Best Location Choose the location with the highest total weighted score
Weighted Score Formula
Total Score = Sum of (Weight_i x Score_i) for all factors

Consider evaluating two locations A and B:

2. Break-Even Analysis (Quantitative)

Compares locations based on fixed and variable costs to determine the most economical choice at different production volumes.

Break-Even Point Formula
BEP (units) = Fixed Cost / (Selling Price - Variable Cost per unit)
BEP = F / (S - V)
Where: F = Fixed Costs, S = Selling Price per unit, V = Variable Cost per unit
Crossover Point (Between Two Locations)
F_A + V_A(X) = F_B + V_B(X)
Solve for X to find the volume at which both locations have equal total cost
Break-Even Location Analysis

Given:

  • Location X: Fixed Cost = Rs.150,000, Variable Cost = Rs.10/unit
  • Location Y: Fixed Cost = Rs.350,000, Variable Cost = Rs.8/unit

Find Crossover Point:

150,000 + 10X = 350,000 + 8X

2X = 200,000

X = 100,000 units

Decision Rule:

  • If demand < 100,000 units: Choose Location X (lower fixed costs)
  • If demand > 100,000 units: Choose Location Y (lower variable costs)
  • For demand of 130,000 units: Location Y is appropriate

3. Centre of Gravity Method

A quantitative technique for finding a location that minimizes total transportation costs to multiple destinations.

Centre of Gravity Formulas
C_x = Sum(d_ix x W_i) / Sum(W_i)
C_y = Sum(d_iy x W_i) / Sum(W_i)
Where: d_ix, d_iy = X and Y coordinates of location i; W_i = Weight/Volume shipped to/from location i
Centre of Gravity Calculation
LocationX-CoordinateY-CoordinateWeight (tons)
Supplier A100200500
Supplier B300100300
Customer C400300200

Calculation:

C_x = (100x500 + 300x300 + 400x200) / (500+300+200) = 220,000/1000 = 220

C_y = (200x500 + 100x300 + 300x200) / (500+300+200) = 190,000/1000 = 190

Optimal Location: (220, 190)

4. Transportation Model

A linear programming approach to determine the optimal pattern of shipments from multiple sources to multiple destinations while minimizing total transportation cost.

When to Use Each Method:
  • Factor Rating: When qualitative factors are important and multiple criteria need evaluation
  • Break-Even Analysis: When comparing locations with different cost structures
  • Centre of Gravity: When minimizing transportation costs to multiple points
  • Transportation Model: When optimizing distribution from multiple sources to multiple destinations

3. Plant Layout

Reference: Lecture 3 - OM

Definition and Significance

Plant Layout

The arrangement of machinery, equipment, and other industrial facilities for achieving quick production at the least cost. It involves the physical arrangement of production facilities to optimize workflow, minimize material handling, and maximize space utilization.

A well-designed layout ensures smooth production flow, reduces material handling costs, improves worker safety, and maximizes the use of available space. Poor layout can lead to production bottlenecks, increased costs, and reduced productivity.

Objectives of Good Plant Layout

Minimize
  • Material handling cost and distance
  • Movement of people and materials
  • Hazards and accidents
  • Production delays and bottlenecks
  • Work-in-progress inventory
  • Floor space requirements
Maximize
  • Production capacity and output
  • Labor efficiency and productivity
  • Employee morale and satisfaction
  • Space utilization effectiveness
  • Flexibility for future changes
  • Equipment utilization

Types of Plant Layout

1. Product Layout (Line Layout)

Product Layout

Machines and equipment are arranged in ONE LINE depending on the sequence of operations required for the product. Materials are fed into the first machine, and the finished product comes out from the last machine in the sequence.

Industries Using Product Layout

Sugarcane Mill, Paper Mill, Automobile Assembly, Chemical Plants, Oil Refineries, Cement Plants, Soft Drink Bottling

Advantages
  • Low material handling cost due to straight-line flow
  • Less work-in-progress inventory
  • Less skill required from workers (specialization)
  • Continuous flow of production
  • Shorter total processing time
  • Better production control
  • Reduced floor space per unit of production
Disadvantages
  • High initial capital investment
  • One machine breakdown affects entire line
  • Slowest machine determines line speed
  • Less flexibility for product changes
  • Monotonous work for employees
  • High supervision costs
  • Difficult to isolate quality problems

2. Process Layout (Functional Layout)

Process Layout

Grouping of like machines and operations in one department. Similar equipment is placed together regardless of the product. Uses general-purpose machines that can handle variety of operations.

Industries Using Process Layout

Job Shops, Machine Shops, Hospitals, Universities, Banks, Automobile Repair Shops, Custom Furniture Manufacturing

Advantages
  • Breakdown of one machine doesn't affect overall productivity
  • High machine utilization
  • Flexible to design and volume changes
  • Variety of products can be produced
  • Lower initial investment in equipment
  • Better employee skill development
  • Easier to handle breakdowns
Disadvantages
  • High material handling cost (backtracking)
  • More time lag between operations
  • High work-in-progress inventory
  • More inspection points required
  • Complex production planning and control
  • Longer throughput time
  • Higher skill requirements

3. Fixed Position Layout

Fixed Position Layout

The product remains stationary, and workers, machines, and materials move TO the product. Used for bulky, heavy, or immobile products where movement is impractical or impossible.

Industries Using Fixed Position Layout

Aircraft Assembly, Ship Building, Building Construction, Dam Construction, Bridge Construction, Large Machine Manufacturing, Locomotive Manufacturing

Advantages
  • Product doesn't need to be moved
  • High flexibility in design changes
  • Workers develop pride in completing entire product
  • Easy to adjust sequence of operations
Disadvantages
  • High movement of workers and materials
  • Duplicate equipment may be needed
  • Complex scheduling required
  • Higher space requirements

4. Cellular Manufacturing (Group Technology Layout)

Cellular Manufacturing

Similar parts are grouped into families to take advantage of similarities in design and production. Machines are grouped into cells, each producing a part family. Combines advantages of both product and process layouts.

When cellular manufacturing is automated, it becomes a Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS), which can produce a variety of products with minimal changeover time.

Advantages
  • Reduced setup time and material handling
  • Reduced work-in-progress inventory
  • Improved quality through specialization
  • Better worker satisfaction (variety)
  • Faster throughput time
Requirements
  • Parts must be classifiable into families
  • Requires analysis of part routings
  • Worker cross-training needed
  • Cell design expertise required

5. Combined Layout (Hybrid Layout)

Combined Layout

A combination of process and product layouts. Useful when items are made in different types and sizes, requiring both specialized and general-purpose equipment.

Combined Layout Example

Soap Manufacturing:

  • Product Layout: Used for main soap production line (mixing, molding, cutting, packaging)
  • Process Layout: Used for ancillary services like heating plant, glycerin recovery, power house

4. Work Study

Reference: Lecture 7 - OM Work Study

Introduction to Work Study

Work Study

A systematic examination of the methods of carrying out activities to improve the effective use of resources and to set standards of performance. It is a key tool for improving productivity.

Work Study Components
Work Study = Method Study + Time Study
Method Study (How to do) + Time Study (How long it takes) = Improved Productivity
Purpose of Work Study:

To improve productivity by analyzing and optimizing work methods (Method Study) and measuring the time required to perform tasks (Time Study). The ultimate goal is to achieve maximum output with minimum resources.

Method Study

Method Study

Systematic recording and critical examination of existing and proposed ways of doing work, as a means of developing and applying easier, more effective methods and reducing costs.

Objectives of Method Study

  • Improve processes and procedures
  • Improve factory, shop, and workplace layout
  • Improve design of plant and equipment
  • Reduce human effort and fatigue
  • Improve use of materials, machines, and manpower
  • Develop better physical working environment
  • Eliminate unnecessary operations

Steps of Method Study (8 Steps)

SELECT Choose the work to be studied based on economic, technical, and human considerations. Priority to bottleneck operations, high-cost processes, or hazardous activities.
RECORD Collect all relevant facts about the existing method using charts, diagrams, and observation. Document everything systematically.
EXAMINE Critically analyze the recorded facts using questioning technique (What? Why? Where? When? Who? How?). Challenge every aspect of the current method.
DEVELOP Develop the most practical, economical, and effective method considering all circumstances. Apply ECRS principle (Eliminate, Combine, Rearrange, Simplify).
EVALUATE Assess alternative solutions and determine cost-effectiveness of each. Consider technical feasibility and human factors.
DEFINE Define the new method clearly in writing. Create standard operating procedures that can be easily understood and followed.
INSTALL Implement the new method by training workers, obtaining acceptance, and making necessary changes to equipment and layout.
MAINTAIN Check regularly to ensure the new method is being followed. Monitor performance and make improvements as needed.

Memory Aid - SREDEVAL: Select, Record, Examine, Develop, Evaluate, Define, Install, Maintain

Time Study

Time Study

A work measurement technique for recording the times and rates of working for the elements of a specified job carried out under specified conditions, and for analyzing the data to determine the time necessary for carrying out the job at a defined level of performance.

Objectives of Time Study

  • Establish time standards for jobs
  • Provide basis for wage incentive schemes
  • Help in production planning and scheduling
  • Estimate labor costs for quotations
  • Determine manpower requirements
  • Compare efficiency of alternative methods

Steps of Time Study

Select the Job Choose the job to be studied. Ensure the method has been standardized through method study first.
Record All Data Document all relevant information about the job, including conditions, equipment, and worker being observed.
Break into Elements Divide the job into clearly defined elements. Each element should be easily identifiable and measurable.
Measure Time Use stopwatch to time each element. Record multiple cycles to ensure accuracy.
Rate Performance Assess the worker's pace relative to standard performance (100%). This is performance rating.
Calculate Standard Time Compile data, calculate basic time, add allowances, and determine standard time.

Time Study Calculations

Basic Time Calculation
Basic Time = Observed Time x (Rating / 100)
Rating is the performance rating given to the worker (100 = standard pace)
Standard Time Calculation
Standard Time = Basic Time + Allowances
OR
Standard Time = Basic Time x (1 + Allowance%)

Types of Allowances

Allowance Type Purpose Typical Range
Personal Allowance For personal needs like drinking water, using washroom, etc. 5-7%
Fatigue Allowance For recovery from physical and mental fatigue 4-10% (varies by job)
Contingency Allowance For unexpected delays like tool breakage, material shortage 2-5%
Special Allowance For startup, shutdown, cleaning, and setup activities Varies
Rework Allowance For reworking spoiled parts due to factors beyond operator's control Varies by process
Time Study Calculation Example

Given:

  • Observed Time = 80 seconds
  • Performance Rating = 80%
  • Total Allowance = 15%

Solution:

Basic Time = 80 x (80/100) = 80 x 0.80 = 64 seconds

Standard Time = 64 x (1 + 0.15) = 64 x 1.15 = 73.6 seconds

5. Value Engineering / Value Analysis

Reference: Lecture 5 - OM VA & ISO

Definition and Concept

Value Engineering (VE) / Value Analysis (VA)

A systematic method to improve the "value" of goods or services by examining their function. It analyzes products or services to achieve essential functions at the lowest overall cost without sacrificing quality, reliability, safety, or performance.

Value Equation
Value = Function / Cost
Higher value is achieved by: (1) Same function at lower cost, OR (2) Better function at same cost, OR (3) Better function at lower cost
Value Engineering vs Value Analysis:
  • Value Engineering: Applied during the design stage of a new product
  • Value Analysis: Applied to existing products to improve value

Both use the same methodology; the difference is timing of application.

Types of Functions

Primary (Basic) Function

The main purpose for which the product/service exists. Without this function, the product would have no reason to exist.

Example: For a pen, the primary function is "to write."

Secondary Functions

Additional features that support the primary function or add aesthetic/convenience value.

Example: For a pen - grip comfort, ink visibility, clip for carrying.

Value Engineering focuses on maintaining the primary function while eliminating or reducing secondary functions that don't add proportional value.

Steps of Value Engineering (Job Plan)

Information Phase Gather complete data about the product - its function, cost breakdown, specifications, requirements. Answer: What is it? What does it do? What does it cost?
Analysis Phase (Function Analysis) Analyze functions systematically. Identify primary and secondary functions. Determine worth of each function. Ask: What should it do? What is each function worth?
Creative Phase (Speculation) Generate alternative ideas using brainstorming and other creative techniques. Quantity of ideas is more important than quality at this stage. No criticism allowed.
Evaluation Phase Evaluate alternatives for technical and economic feasibility. Screen ideas based on potential value improvement, risk, and implementation difficulty.
Development Phase Develop selected alternatives into workable solutions. Prepare detailed designs, calculations, and cost estimates for promising ideas.
Presentation Phase Present recommendations to management with complete cost-benefit analysis. Include implementation plan and expected savings.
Implementation Phase Implement approved changes. Monitor results and verify that expected value improvements are achieved.

Brainstorming in Value Engineering

Brainstorming

A group creativity technique designed to generate a large number of ideas for solving a problem. It is crucial in the Creative Phase of Value Engineering.

Rules of Brainstorming

  1. No Criticism: All ideas are accepted without judgment during the session
  2. Wild Ideas Welcome: Encourage unconventional, even seemingly impossible ideas
  3. Quantity Wanted: Generate as many ideas as possible; quantity leads to quality
  4. Combination and Improvement: Build on others' ideas, combine and improve them

Brainstorming sessions should have 6-12 participants from diverse backgrounds, last 30-60 minutes, and be facilitated by a trained leader who ensures rules are followed.

Benefits of Value Engineering

Direct Benefits
  • Reduced product costs
  • Improved product quality
  • Better resource utilization
  • Increased profitability
  • Competitive advantage
Indirect Benefits
  • Enhanced team collaboration
  • Improved creative thinking skills
  • Better understanding of customer needs
  • Systematic problem-solving culture
  • Continuous improvement mindset

7. Material Requirements Planning (MRP)

Reference: Lecture 7 - OM MRP

Definition and Concept

Material Requirements Planning (MRP)

A computerized inventory management system that uses bill of materials (BOM), inventory data, and master production schedule (MPS) to calculate requirements for materials. MRP determines WHAT to order, HOW MUCH to order, WHEN to order, and WHEN to schedule delivery.

MRP vs Traditional Inventory Systems:

Traditional systems (like EOQ) treat demand as independent and constant. MRP recognizes that demand for components is DEPENDENT on demand for finished products and is often LUMPY (irregular, in batches) rather than smooth.

Objectives of MRP

  1. Determine Requirements: Calculate what components are needed to meet the Master Production Schedule
  2. Keep Priorities Current: Continuously update priorities - add, delete, expedite, delay, or change orders based on changing conditions
  3. Right Material: Ensure the right materials are available
  4. Right Quantity: In the right quantity
  5. Right Time: At the right time
  6. Minimize Inventory: Avoid excess stock while preventing shortages

Inputs to MRP System

1. Master Production Schedule (MPS)
  • Statement of end items to be produced
  • Quantity of each product needed
  • Completion dates for each
  • Based on customer orders and sales forecast
  • Represents firm production commitments
2. Bill of Materials (BOM)
  • Structured list of all components
  • Part numbers and descriptions
  • Quantity required per parent
  • Material specifications
  • Shows parent-component relationships
3. Inventory Records
  • On-hand quantities
  • On-order quantities (scheduled receipts)
  • Allocated quantities
  • Lead times
  • Safety stock levels
  • Lot sizing rules

Key MRP Terminology

Term Definition Example
Dependent Demand Demand that is derived from demand for another item (parent-child relationship) Demand for wheels depends on demand for cars
Independent Demand Demand that is not related to demand for any other item Demand for finished cars from customers
Lumpy Demand Sudden or large quantity demand to make a batch of final product Need 1000 engines when car batch is scheduled
Lead Time Time from order placement to receipt (ordering lead time) or time to manufacture (manufacturing lead time) 2 weeks to receive from supplier
Gross Requirement Total quantity needed before considering inventory Need 500 units total
Net Requirement Quantity needed after subtracting available inventory 500 - 200 on hand = 300 net requirement
Planned Order Receipt Quantity expected to be received when a planned order is executed Order for 300 units arriving in week 5
Planned Order Release Quantity and timing of orders to be placed (offset by lead time) Release order in week 3 for week 5 receipt
Net Requirements Calculation
Net Requirement = Gross Requirement - (On Hand - Safety Stock - Allocated)

How MRP Works - The Process

Bucketing Consolidate requirements into time periods (usually weeks). Group all demands occurring within a period.
Part Explosion Divide items into their constituent materials using the BOM. Determine requirements for all component levels.
Offsetting Displace (offset) requirement by lead time. If item is needed in week 10 with 2-week lead time, order must be released by week 8.
Aggregation Find gross requirements by combining all demands for a component from different parent items.
Netting Calculate net requirements by subtracting on-hand inventory and scheduled receipts from gross requirements.
Lot Sizing Consolidate net requirements into economic order quantities using lot-sizing rules (lot-for-lot, EOQ, POQ, etc.).

MRP Outputs

Primary Outputs
  • Planned Order Schedule: Quantity and timing of material orders and production orders
  • Order Releases: Authorization to execute planned orders
  • Changes: Modifications to planned orders - expedite, delay, or cancel
Secondary Outputs
  • Planning Reports: Forecasts, budgets, inventory projections
  • Performance Reports: Measuring system effectiveness
  • Exception Reports: Alerts for problems - late orders, shortages, excess inventory

Benefits and Limitations of MRP

Benefits
  • Materials available when needed
  • Minimized inventory levels
  • Reduced customer lead times
  • Increased manufacturing efficiency
  • Better capacity utilization
  • Improved coordination between departments
  • Better response to demand changes
  • Reduced expediting and firefighting
Limitations
  • Expensive to implement
  • Requires accurate data inputs
  • Dependent on forecast accuracy
  • Assumes infinite capacity
  • Lack of flexibility in some situations
  • Requires discipline in data maintenance
  • Complex to understand and operate
  • May create nervousness (frequent changes)

What is Inventory?

Inventory

Stock of items kept to meet future demand. Purpose of inventory management is to determine how many units to order and when to order.

Inventory Control

Planned approach of determining what to order, when to order, how much to order, and how much to stock so that costs associated with buying and storing are optimal without interrupting production or sales.

Selective Inventory Control - ABC Analysis

ABC Analysis (Always Better Control)

Classification of inventory items based on their annual usage value (cost x volume). Based on the Pareto principle (80-20 rule), it helps focus management attention on the most important items.

Class % of Items % of Value Control Level Ordering Policy
A 5-15% 70-80% Very Tight Control Frequent ordering, low safety stock, accurate forecasting, close monitoring
B 30% 15% Normal Control Periodic review, moderate safety stock, regular monitoring
C 50-60% 5-10% Simple Control Large orders, high safety stock, simple systems, infrequent review
ABC Analysis Calculation

Steps to perform ABC Analysis:

  1. Calculate annual usage value for each item: Unit Cost x Annual Usage
  2. Rank items in descending order of usage value
  3. Calculate cumulative percentages
  4. Classify: First 70-80% of value = A, Next 15% = B, Remaining = C

Advantages of ABC Analysis

  • Helps manager focus attention on few important items (A class)
  • Shows results quickly by controlling high-value items
  • Reduces clerical costs and saves time
  • Results in better planning and control

Limitation: ABC Analysis is not a one-time exercise. Items need to be reviewed and recategorized periodically as costs and usage patterns change.

VED Analysis

VED Analysis (Vital, Essential, Desirable)

Classification based on the criticality of items to production rather than their monetary value. Particularly useful for spare parts inventory management.

Category Criticality Stock-out Impact Control Policy
V - Vital Absolutely Critical Production STOPS completely Always keep in stock, may duplicate
E - Essential Very Important Stock-out costs are VERY HIGH Maintain adequate safety stock
D - Desirable Nice to Have No immediate stoppage, nominal costs Order when needed, minimal stock

ABC-VED Matrix

Combining ABC and VED analysis provides a comprehensive view for inventory control decisions:

  • Category I (Highest Priority): AV, AE, AD, BV, CV - Critical items requiring maximum attention
  • Category II (Moderate Priority): BE, CE, BD - Moderate control
  • Category III (Low Priority): CD - Minimal control needed

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) Model

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

The optimal order quantity that minimizes total inventory cost by balancing ordering costs and carrying costs.

Assumptions of Basic EOQ Model

  • Demand is known with certainty and constant over time
  • No shortages are allowed
  • Lead time for receipt of orders is constant
  • Order quantity is received all at once (instantaneous replenishment)
  • No quantity discounts available
  • Only two costs: ordering cost and carrying cost
EOQ Formula
EOQ = SQRT(2 x D x Co / Cc)
Where: D = Annual Demand, Co = Ordering Cost per order, Cc = Annual Carrying Cost per unit
Total Inventory Cost
Total Cost = (Co x D/Q) + (Cc x Q/2)
Ordering Cost + Carrying Cost
Where Q = Order Quantity, D/Q = Number of orders per year, Q/2 = Average inventory
EOQ Calculation Example

Given:

  • Annual Demand (D) = 10,000 units
  • Ordering Cost (Co) = Rs. 50 per order
  • Carrying Cost (Cc) = Rs. 2 per unit per year

Solution:

EOQ = SQRT(2 x 10,000 x 50 / 2)

EOQ = SQRT(500,000)

EOQ = 707 units

Number of orders per year = 10,000/707 = 14.1 orders

Total Cost = (50 x 10000/707) + (2 x 707/2) = Rs. 707 + Rs. 707 = Rs. 1,414

Quantity Discounts

When suppliers offer price discounts for ordering larger quantities, the EOQ decision becomes more complex because:

  • Lower unit cost is attractive
  • But larger order means higher carrying cost
  • Trade-off between reduced purchase cost and increased carrying cost

Procedure for Quantity Discount Analysis

  1. Calculate EOQ for each price level
  2. If EOQ falls within a valid price range, calculate total cost
  3. If EOQ falls outside valid range, adjust to nearest valid quantity
  4. Calculate total cost for each price break point
  5. Compare total costs and select minimum
Total Cost with Purchase Price
Total Cost = Purchase Cost + Ordering Cost + Carrying Cost
TC = P x D + (Co x D/Q) + (Cc x Q/2)
Where P = Unit purchase price

9. Sequencing

Reference: Lecture 9 - OM Sequencing

Definition and Concept

Sequencing

Determining the order in which jobs are to be processed on machines to minimize total elapsed time, idle time, and optimize resource utilization. The goal is to find the optimal sequence that results in minimum total completion time.

Key Terminology

  • Processing Time: Time required to process a job on a machine
  • Total Elapsed Time: Time from start of first job to completion of last job
  • Idle Time: Time a machine sits idle waiting for work
  • Makespan: Total time to complete all jobs
  • Flow Time: Time a job spends in the system

Johnson's Rule (n Jobs, 2 Machines)

Johnson's Rule

An algorithm to find the optimal sequence for processing n jobs on 2 machines (A and B) where all jobs must follow the same sequence AB (first processed on A, then on B).

Steps of Johnson's Rule

List All Jobs List all jobs with their processing times on both machines A and B.
Find Minimum Time Find the minimum processing time among all jobs on both machines.
Place the Job If minimum is on Machine A: Place job FIRST in sequence. If minimum is on Machine B: Place job LAST in sequence. (If tie, either rule can apply)
Remove and Repeat Remove the scheduled job from the list and repeat steps 2-3 until all jobs are sequenced.
Calculate Elapsed Time Create a timeline showing start and end times for each job on each machine. Calculate total elapsed time and idle times.
Johnson's Rule Example

Given: 5 jobs to be processed on machines A then B

JobMachine AMachine B
J123
J246
J351
J474
J518

Solution:

  • Minimum = 1 (J5 on A) -> J5 goes FIRST
  • Next minimum = 1 (J3 on B) -> J3 goes LAST
  • Next minimum = 2 (J1 on A) -> J1 goes second (first available from front)
  • Next minimum = 4 (J2 on A or J4 on B) -> J2 next from front, J4 before J3

Optimal Sequence: J5 - J1 - J2 - J4 - J3

Extended Johnson's Rule (n Jobs, 3 Machines)

Extended Johnson's Rule

For 3 machines (A, B, C) in sequence ABC, Johnson's rule can be applied if certain conditions are met.

Applicability Conditions

The extended rule can be applied when at least one of these conditions is true:

  • Condition 1: Minimum time on Machine A >= Maximum time on Machine B
  • Condition 2: Minimum time on Machine C >= Maximum time on Machine B

Conversion Method

Convert the 3-machine problem to a 2-machine problem:

  • Machine G: Machine A time + Machine B time
  • Machine H: Machine B time + Machine C time

Then apply standard Johnson's rule to machines G and H.

Graphical Method (2 Jobs, Multiple Machines)

Used when 2 jobs need to be processed on multiple machines, possibly in different sequences.

Steps for Graphical Method

  1. Draw X-axis for Job 1 times, Y-axis for Job 2 times
  2. Mark processing blocks for each machine (showing when both jobs need the same machine)
  3. Shade blocked regions where both jobs would use the same machine
  4. Find the path from origin to completion that avoids blocked regions with minimum horizontal + vertical movement
  5. Calculate total elapsed time from the path
Graphical Method Result

For 2 jobs on 5 machines with different sequences:

The optimal schedule might result in:

  • Machine A: J1 first, then J2
  • Machine B: J2 first, then J1
  • Machine C: J2 first, then J1
  • Machine D: J2 first, then J1
  • Machine E: J2 first, then J1

Understanding Quality

Quality

Fitness for purpose at lowest cost. The degree to which a product fulfills the requirements of the customer. Quality is the totality of features and characteristics for both products and services that can satisfy both explicit and implicit customer needs.

Quality Control

Systematic control of various factors that affect the quality of the product, including materials, tools, machines, type of labor, working conditions, and measuring instruments.

Factors Affecting Quality (5 M's + More)

  • Men: Skills, training, motivation of workers
  • Materials: Quality and consistency of raw materials
  • Machines: Condition, capability, and precision of equipment
  • Manufacturing Conditions: Environment, processes, methods
  • Money: Investment capability
  • Management: Policies for quality level

Statistical Quality Control

Statistical Quality Control (SQC)

A quality control system employing statistical techniques to control, improve, and maintain quality through inspection, testing, and analysis. SQC uses data from samples to make decisions about the quality of entire batches or processes.

Divisions of SQC

Category Purpose Tools Used
Descriptive Statistics Describe quality characteristics and relationships Mean, Standard Deviation, Range, Histograms
SPC (Statistical Process Control) Inspect random samples, decide if process is in control Control Charts (X-bar, R, p, c charts)
Acceptance Sampling Randomly inspect samples, accept/reject entire lot Sampling Plans, OC Curves

Causes of Variation

Variation in manufactured products is inevitable. No two products are identical. The goal is to understand and control variation.

Chance Causes (Common/Random)
  • Natural/inherent variation in the process
  • Aged machines and equipment
  • Material variability within specifications
  • Poor lighting, temperature fluctuations
  • Normal variation in worker performance
  • Management responsibility to reduce
  • Always present, creates predictable pattern
Assignable Causes (Special/Non-random)
  • Due to specific changes in material, machine, operator
  • Machine improper setting or breakdown
  • Defective raw material batch
  • New, untrained worker
  • Tool wear
  • Worker/supervisor can identify and eliminate
  • Causes abnormal variation patterns

Key Distinction: A process with only chance causes is "in control" and predictable. When assignable causes are present, the process is "out of control" and requires investigation.

Control Charts

Control Charts

Graphical representations based on statistical sampling theory that detect variations in processes and warn of departures from tolerance limits. They distinguish between common cause and special cause variation.

Components of a Control Chart

  • Center Line (CL): Average value or standard value (solid line)
  • Upper Control Limit (UCL): Upper boundary of acceptable variation (dotted line)
  • Lower Control Limit (LCL): Lower boundary of acceptable variation (dotted line)
3-Sigma Control Limits
UCL = Mean + 3 sigma
LCL = Mean - 3 sigma
Data beyond 3-sigma represents less than 0.3% of all points if process is in control

Types of Control Charts

For Variables (Measurable) For Attributes (Countable)
X-bar Chart: Monitors process mean p-Chart: Proportion/fraction defective
R Chart: Monitors process range (variability) np-Chart: Number of defectives (constant sample size)
sigma Chart: Monitors standard deviation c-Chart: Number of defects per unit
Variables: weight, length, temperature, etc. | Attributes: good/bad, defective/acceptable, number of defects

Interpreting Control Charts

Signs of Out-of-Control Process

  • Point beyond control limits
  • 7 or more points in a row on one side of center line (Run)
  • 7 or more points trending consistently up or down (Trend)
  • Unusual patterns (cycles, clusters)
  • 2 of 3 consecutive points beyond 2-sigma
  • 4 of 5 consecutive points beyond 1-sigma

Advantages of Control Charts

  • Indicates if process is in control or out of control
  • Determines process variability
  • Ensures product quality level
  • Provides early warning, reduces scrap and rejection
  • Builds organization reputation through consistent quality
  • Provides data for process improvement

SQC vs 100% Inspection

Advantages of SQC over 100% Inspection
  • Cost Reduction: Only sample is inspected, not all items
  • Greater Efficiency: Less time, less inspector fatigue
  • Easy to Apply: Once plan established, can be used by non-specialists
  • Accurate Prediction: Future quality can be predicted statistically
  • Destructive Testing: Only option when testing destroys product
  • Early Detection: Problems caught before large quantities produced
When 100% Inspection is Needed
  • Critical safety components
  • Very expensive items
  • Small lot sizes
  • When cost of defect reaching customer is extreme
  • Regulated industries requiring full inspection

What is Lean Management?

Lean Management

A customer-focused waste elimination method that makes the process and thereby organization "lean" (eliminating fat). It is a long-term approach that systematically seeks small, incremental changes in processes to improve efficiency and quality.

The goal of lean thinking is the creation of a continuous stream that delivers customer value with the least waste of resources within the shortest possible time.

Lean Philosophy Principles

  • Continuous Improvement: A culture practiced at every level by every team
  • Scientific Method: Application of systematic study of work processes
  • Respect for People: Respect for customers and those who do the work
  • Waste Elimination: Distinguishing value-adding work from non-value-adding work
  • Quality and Safety: Assuring quality and safety of all work
  • Process Focus: Improving work processes, not blaming people
  • Teamwork: Shared responsibility that cuts through organizational walls
  • Flow: Interruption-free process from beginning to end

The 3 M's of Lean

Japanese Term English Meaning Focus Area

Mura Inconsistency/Unevenness Variation that increases unpredictability - to be smoothed out
Muri Unreasonableness/Overburden Excessive stress on people or equipment - to be balanced

7 Types of Waste (Muda) - TIMWOOD

  1. T - Transportation: Unnecessary movement of materials
  2. I - Inventory: Excess stock beyond immediate needs
  3. M - Motion: Unnecessary movement of people
  4. W - Waiting: Delays and idle time
  5. O - Over-production: Making more than needed or too early
  6. O - Over-processing: Doing more work than required
  7. D - Defects: Making defective items requiring rework

8th Waste: Many experts add "Non-utilized Talent" - failing to use employees' skills, knowledge, and creativity.

Japanese English Meaning Action

Five Steps of Lean Implementation

Create Flow Make remaining value-creating steps flow. Eliminate functional barriers and develop product-focused organization that dramatically improves lead time.

13. Theory of Constraints (TOC)

Reference: Lean Management and TOC

What is Theory of Constraints?

Theory of Constraints (TOC)

A methodology for identifying the most important limiting factor (constraint or bottleneck) that stands in the way of achieving a goal, and then systematically improving that constraint until it is no longer the limiting factor.

Core Concept:

Every complex system, including manufacturing processes, consists of multiple linked activities. ONE of these acts as a constraint upon the entire system - the "weakest link in the chain." Total system throughput can only be improved when the constraint is improved.

Developed by Dr. Eliyahu Goldratt and introduced in his 1984 bestselling novel "The Goal."

The Five Focusing Steps

IDENTIFY the Constraint Find the system's constraint - the bottleneck that limits throughput. This is the weakest link determining system performance.
EXPLOIT the Constraint Maximize output from the constraint with existing resources. Ensure the constraint is never idle, never works on defective products, and operates at full capacity.
SUBORDINATE Everything Else Align all other processes to support the constraint. Non-constraints should not produce more than the constraint can handle. The system marches to the constraint's drum.
ELEVATE the Constraint If constraint still exists after exploitation, add capacity. Invest in additional resources, equipment, or shifts for the constraint resource.
REPEAT - Don't Let Inertia Become Constraint Once constraint is broken, return to step 1. A new constraint will emerge. Continuous improvement is essential.

Key Principle: Spending time optimizing non-constraints will not provide significant benefits. Only improvements to the constraint will further the goal of more profit.

Throughput Accounting

TOC uses an alternative accounting methodology called Throughput Accounting that eliminates harmful distortions from traditional accounting.

Key Difference from Traditional Accounting

  • Traditional: Inventory is an ASSET (can be sold for cash)
  • TOC: Inventory is a LIABILITY (ties up cash, incurs holding costs)

Core Measures

  • Throughput (T): Rate at which the system generates money through sales (Revenue - Truly Variable Costs)
  • Investment (I): Money tied up in the system (inventory, equipment)
  • Operating Expense (OE): Money spent to turn Investment into Throughput
Throughput Accounting Formulas
Net Profit = Throughput - Operating Expenses
ROI = Net Profit / Investment
Productivity = Throughput / Operating Expenses
Investment Turns = Throughput / Investment

TOC Decision Priority (In Order)

  1. Increase Throughput (highest priority - no upper limit)
  2. Reduce Investment
  3. Reduce Operating Expenses (limited by zero)
Focus on Sales, Not Cost Cutting:

TOC emphasizes that cutting expenses is limited (you can't go below zero), but increasing throughput has no such limitation. Focus more on building sales than on cutting costs.

Benefits of TOC

Primary Benefits
  • Increased Profit: The primary goal
  • Fast Improvement: Focused attention on one critical area
  • Improved Capacity: More product manufactured
  • Reduced Lead Times: Smoother, faster product flow
  • Reduced Inventory: Less work-in-process
Secondary Benefits
  • Clear focus for management decisions
  • Improved coordination between departments
  • Better understanding of system dynamics
  • Prioritized improvement activities
  • Scientific approach to problem-solving

14. Demand Forecasting

Reference: Demand Forecasting

What is Demand Forecasting?

Demand Forecasting

The process of predicting future demand for products or services using historical data, market analysis, and various quantitative and qualitative techniques. Forecasting is the foundation for planning in operations management.

Importance of Forecasting

  • Basis for capacity planning
  • Production scheduling
  • Inventory management
  • Workforce planning
  • Budget planning
  • Supply chain coordination

Types of Forecasting Methods

1. Qualitative Methods (Judgmental)

Used when historical data is limited or unavailable, for new products, or when expert judgment is needed.

Method Description When to Use
Delphi Method Expert panel gives anonymous opinions, iteratively refined through multiple rounds until consensus Long-term forecasting, new technology, strategic decisions
Market Research Surveys, interviews, focus groups to gather customer insights New product launches, market entry
Sales Force Composite Sales team estimates compiled and adjusted B2B products, regional forecasts
Executive Opinion Management judgment combined (jury of executive opinion) Strategic planning, quick estimates

2. Quantitative Methods (Statistical)

Used when historical data is available and patterns can be identified.

Method Formula/Description Best For
Simple Moving Average MA = Sum of last n periods / n Stable demand, short-term
Weighted Moving Average WMA = Sum of (Weight x Demand) / Sum of Weights When recent data is more relevant
Exponential Smoothing F_t = F_(t-1) + alpha(A_(t-1) - F_(t-1)) Simple, responsive to changes
Linear Regression Y = a + bX (trend line based on historical data) When clear trend exists

Key Forecasting Formulas

Simple Moving Average
MA_n = (D_1 + D_2 + ... + D_n) / n
Where D = Demand in each period, n = number of periods
Exponential Smoothing
F_t = F_(t-1) + alpha(A_(t-1) - F_(t-1))
OR: F_t = alpha(A_(t-1)) + (1-alpha)(F_(t-1))
Where: F = Forecast, A = Actual, alpha = Smoothing constant (0 to 1)
Forecast Error Measures
MAD = Sum|Actual - Forecast| / n
MSE = Sum(Actual - Forecast)^2 / n
MAPE = (Sum|Error|/Actual) x 100 / n
MAD = Mean Absolute Deviation, MSE = Mean Squared Error, MAPE = Mean Absolute Percentage Error
Choosing Alpha (alpha) in Exponential Smoothing:
  • Low alpha (0.1-0.3): Smooths out fluctuations, slow to respond to changes. Use for stable demand.
  • High alpha (0.4-0.5): More responsive to recent changes. Use for volatile demand or when recent data is more relevant.

Factors Affecting Forecast Accuracy

  • Data Quality: Availability and accuracy of historical data
  • Time Horizon: Short-term forecasts are more accurate than long-term
  • Product Life Cycle: New products harder to forecast than mature ones
  • Market Conditions: Stability of competitive and economic environment
  • Seasonality: Presence and predictability of seasonal patterns
  • Aggregation Level: Aggregate forecasts more accurate than individual SKU forecasts
