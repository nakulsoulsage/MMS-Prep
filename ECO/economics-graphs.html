1. Demand & Elasticity

1.1 Basic Demand Curve (Marshallian Model)

Axes: Y-axis: Price (P) | X-axis: Quantity (Q)
Curve: D (Downward sloping)
Key Features: The demand curve shows an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded. As price increases, quantity demanded decreases (Law of Demand). The curve incorporates the time element (market, short run, long run) and shows the role of elasticity in price adjustments.

1.2 Five Degrees of Price Elasticity of Demand

Formula: edp = (%ΔD) / (%ΔP) = (ΔD/ΔP) × (P0/D0) = m(P/D) = tan θ (P/D)

Perfectly Inelastic (edp = 0)

Eg: Pharma (life-saving drugs)

Relatively Inelastic (edp < 1)

Eg: Necessities (salt, sugar)

Unitary Elastic (edp = 1)

Theoretical case only

Relatively Elastic (edp > 1)

Eg: Luxuries

Perfectly Elastic (edp = ∞)

Perfect Competition

Interpretation: RIE is good when price rises (demand falls but not much). RIE is bad when price falls (even though price drops, demand drops a bit). RE is bad when price rises (market is emptying). RE is good when price falls (market is increasing).

1.3 Cross Elasticity of Demand

Formula: edxy = (%ΔDx) / (%ΔPy) = (ΔDx/ΔPy) × (Py0/Dx0) = m(Py/Dx)

Substitutes (edxy > 0)

x Py +ve ↑Py → ↓Dy → ↑Dx

Positive relationship (Rival goods)

Complements (edxy < 0)

x Py -ve ↑Py → ↓Dy → ↓Dx

Negative relationship (Supportive goods)

TINA Factor: If there's a TINA (There Is No Alternative) factor, Dx = 0, edxy = 0, market is monopolized (perfectly inelastic). When there is no TINA factor, there is competition. Supportive goods constantly need to be innovated to survive when main good is getting a beating.

1.4 Bandwagon Effect & Snob Effect

Bandwagon Effect (DBW)

1 D2 D3 DBW More elastic than individual demand

Consumers buy more because others are buying (herd behavior)

Snob Effect (DSG)

1 D2 D3 DSG More inelastic than individual demand

Consumers buy less because others are buying (exclusivity)

2. Production Analysis

2.1 TPP, APP, MPP Curves (Law of Variable Proportions)

Axes: X-axis: Quantity of Labour (QL) | Y-axis: Output (TPP/APP/MPP)
Formulas: TPPn = ∫MPP(dQ) | APPn = TPPn/Qn | MPPn = d(TPPn)/d(Qn)
L TPP Mechanical Freshness Mechanical Fatigue Mechanical Exhaustion TPP Max TPP
L APP/MPP APP MPP MPP=0 MPP=APP
Law of Variable Proportions: In the short term, technology does not change. Marginal shapes the Total. When T is at peak (maxima), M becomes zero. The three stages represent: (1) Mechanical Freshness - warranty period, (2) Mechanical Fatigue - repair/replace period, (3) Mechanical Exhaustion - recycle/salvage period.

2.2 Isoquant Curves (Isoquant Map)

Axes: X-axis: Labour (L) | Y-axis: Capital (K)
Curves: IQ1, IQ2, IQ3 (representing different output levels: 10, 20, 30 units)
3;30 IQ2;20 IQ1;10 Higher output
Isoquant Map: Represents the willingness of the producer to produce a certain quantity of output. Isoquant is locus of varying quantities of L and Capital (K) showing the same level of output. Higher isoquants represent higher levels of production.

2.3 Factor Cost Line & Producer's Equilibrium

Axes: X-axis: Labour | Y-axis: Capital
Lines: C1, C2, C3 (Factor Cost Lines at different budget levels)
1 C2 C3 IQ0 IQ1 IQ2 E1 E2 E3 Expansion Path A B G (aspirational)
Producer's Equilibrium: At point E, producer's ability and willingness are compatible. E is the tangent for the isoquant, i.e., the willingness = slope of ability. G is aspirational (out of budget). A and B are irrational as with same cost as E, less is produced. Expansion Path: Trajectory of production that a firm adopts, given its willingness and ability to produce as well as the state of technology.

2.4 Returns to Scale (Long Run)

Axes: X-axis: Labour | Y-axis: Capital
Types: IRS (Increasing), CRS (Constant), DRS (Decreasing)

Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS)

Constant Returns to Scale (CRS)

Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS)

Note: DRS emerges when a very superior technology is being made operational when the appropriate support systems (infrastructure) are not in place. IRS creates negative trajectory (reducing gaps) and DRS causes upward slope (increasing gaps). The LAC is shaped by the operations of Laws of Returns to Scale.

3. Cost Curves

3.1 Total Fixed Cost (TFC), Total Variable Cost (TVC), Total Cost (TC)

Axes: X-axis: Quantity (Output) | Y-axis: Cost
Formulas: TC = TFC + TVC | TVC' = MC | TC = ∫MC(dq)
1 Q2 Q3 Q4
Key Relationships: TFC ≠ f(Q) - Fixed cost doesn't change with output. TVC = f(Q) - Variable cost changes with output level. TC curve shares the vertical intercept with TFC (TC curve starts at point F). TC curve is shaped by TVC, i.e., TVC' = MC. TFC is the gap between TVC and TC. Initiating cost (a) is reflected in TVC starting position.

3.2 Average Fixed Cost (AFC)

Formula: AFC = TFC/Q
Properties: Rectangular Hyperbola, Asymptotic
AFC Curve Features: (1) It's a Rectangular Hyperbola. (2) Asymptotic - AFC cannot be zero because the numerator can never be zero. Also, Qty cannot be zero due to mechanical exhaustion, production stops. The AFC continuously falls as output increases.

3.3 Average Variable Cost (AVC)

Formula: AVC = TVC/Q
Shape: Nearly J-shaped (U-shaped)

3.4 Average Cost (AC) and Marginal Cost (MC) Relationship

Formulas: AC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC | MC = d(TC)/d(Q)
Key Relationship: MC intersects AC at AC's minimum (optimal output)
2=A2 (Optimal Output) Sub-optimal output Post-optimal output A1 M1 A3 M3 MC < AC MC > AC
Three Definite Relationships: (1) At optimal output: MC = AC at AC's minima. (2) Before optimal (sub-optimal): AC continuously falls due to mechanical freshness; MC falls and rises; MC lies below AC. (3) After optimal (post-optimal): Both AC and MC continuously rise; MC lies above AC. The MC curve is indicative of the fixed factor efficiency: Efficiency of MC1 > MC (if MC1 curve is lower).

3.5 Excess Capacity / Idle Capacity

Formula: Excess Capacity = OQ2 - OQ1 = Optimum Qty - Actual Qty
1 Q2 Excess Capacity
Interpretation: Tragedy: Leads to inflationary pressures, aggravates unemployment, causes large scale resource wastage. Strategy: Modern companies use excess capacity strategically for production agility, seasonality compliance (minimal inventories), and scientific inventory management.

3.6 Long Run Average Cost (LAC) - Envelope Curve

Concept: LAC is the locus of operational points of every adopted technology
Also called: Envelope Curve, Planning Curve
1 SAC2 SAC3 SAC4 LAC A IRS (Reducing gaps) DRS (Increasing gaps)
Key Points: One short run Avg. cost curve represents one technology. Every successive technology is more cost effective than the previous one. LAC is shaped by laws of Return to Scale with IRS creating negative trajectory and DRS causing upward slope. Point A is concurrent point between technologies. Positive externalities shift LAC downwards (LAC2 to LAC1). Negative externalities shift LAC upwards.

4. Revenue Analysis

4.1 TR, AR, MR under Perfect Competition

Formulas: TR = AR × Q | AR = TR/Q = P | MR = TR' = d(TR)/d(Q)
Key Feature: AR = MR = P (constant price)
c/MRc P
Under Perfect Competition: Prices are uniform, AR = Price. Since TR function is R = 30Q (linear), the corresponding curve will be linear. The slope of AR and MR curves = 0. AR and MR curves are concurrent at market determined price. Such firms can opt for profit maximization or breakeven status but they cannot opt for sales maximization.

4.2 TR, AR, MR under Imperfect Competition

Key Features: TR is inverted U-shaped | AR is downward sloping (= Demand curve) | MR is downward sloping (steeper than AR)
Relationship: Slope of MR = 2 × Slope of AR (linear case)
c ARc MRc MR = 0 MR bisects AR horizontally (50-50 rule for linear AR)
Key Relationships: AR curve is the demand curve. MR can be >0, =0, <0. When TR peaks, MR = 0. MR = a - 2kq (derivative of TR). The MR intersects the horizontal axis at more than mid point (for linear AR) or at less than mid point (depending on the curvature). edp = AR/(AR-MR). Sales maximization is possible only under imperfect competition because in perfect competition, the TR function does not have a maximum position.

Concave AR (MR intersects at less than mid pt)

5. Perfect Competition

5.1 Normal Profit (Breakeven) - TR = TC, π = 0

Condition: MC = MR and slope of MC > slope of MR
At Equilibrium: TR = TC, Total Profit (π) = zero

Industry

d,Qs Price S D Ei P

Firm

At point E: The firm reaches stable equilibrium since MC = MR and the slope of MC > Slope of MR. At price OP, the firm produces OQ units. TR [A(□OPEQ)] = TC [A(□OPEQ)] so π = zero. Many NGOs and charitable institutions perform as not-for-profit firms.

5.2 Supernormal Profit (π > 0)

Condition: MC = MR = AR > AC
Result: TR > TC, firm earns supernormal (positive) profit

Industry

d,Qs Price S D Ei P

Firm

Analysis: At stable equilibrium point E: MC = MR = AR > AC. The firm's total expenditure (at stable eq.) is A(□OTCQ) = TC, but with market prices being OP, which is more than OT by PT, the firm's overall supernormal profit = A(□TPEC). These are the usual commercial entities that ensure their revenues more than cover costs. Supernormal profit would be subject to deductions like CIT (22%), CSR (2%), dividends, and reinvestment.

5.3 Subnormal Profit - Continue (π < 0, AR > AVC)

Condition: MC = MR = AC < C (but AR > AVC)
Decision: Continue production (covers variable costs partially covers fixed costs)

Industry

d,Qs Price S D Ei P

Firm

Analysis: At stable eq. point E: MC = MR = AC < C (AR > AVC). Total cost (at stable eq.) is A(□OTCQ) = TC, which is greater than Total Revenue [A(□OPEQ) = TR]. The subnormal profit = A(□TPEC). Since AR > AVC (Price EQ > variable cost VQ), this firm is able to cover OPEX and partly covers CAPEX. The firm would be advised to continue production hoping that when other firms shut down, the revenue situation improves. This is a financially sick company and will not be allowed to file for bankruptcy.

5.4 Subnormal Profit - Shutdown (π < 0, AR < AVC)

Condition: AR < AVC (Price < Variable Cost)
Decision: Shutdown (cannot cover even variable costs)

Industry

d,Qs Price S D E P

Firm

Analysis: The firm's total cost is as high as OTCQ and revenue is as low as A(□OPEQ). The huge subnormal profit A(□TPEC) would compel this firm to shut down. Since AVC (QV) is more than the price QE, such firms are counselled to shut down since their AVC is more than the price. Such firms would be legally allowed to file for bankruptcy. Example: Mumbai Mills.

5.5 Firm Supply Curve Derivation

Key Insight: The upward sloping MC curve above AVC is the supply curve of the firm.
Relationship: Supply is direct function of price; Price is direct function of cost

Production Efficiency: The slopes of individual firms' supply curve is the same as the slopes of MC curves. If slopes are the same, these firms have the same yellow path - equally efficient, equally productive. If the slope of MC differs (firm C* steeper), less efficient is that firm. Gentler the slopedness of MC curve (firm B*), more efficient is that firm. The summation of each firm's supply is indicative of industry's supply situation.

6. Monopoly

6.1 Monopoly Operations under Different Profit Conditions

Definition: Single seller situation with no close substitutes (TINA - There Is No Alternative)
Equilibrium: MR = MC, slope of MC > slope of MR

Case 1: Supernormal Profit (Short Run)

Case 2: Normal Profit (Short Run)

Case 3: Sub-Normal Profit (Short Run)

Monopoly Analysis: Supernormal profit is expected due to TINA factor and commercial power (urban, conspicuous consumption areas). Normal profit occurs in peri-urban space where consumption can be impacted through demonstration effect. Sub-normal profit: monopoly firm engages with low purchasing power segment in anticipation that consumption basket would normalize the consumption of this good. Supernormal profits will persist in the long run due to TINA factor continuation - ensured by IPR (Intellectual Property Rights).

6.2 Monopoly Long Run Operation

1 MC1 AC2 MC2 AR MR E1 E2 P1 P2 R1 R2 Q1 Q2 Over time: AC1 → AC2 (newer tech), P1 → P2 (reduced), creates illusion of 'up-to-date' good
Long Run Monopoly: The demand for the monopoly firm's commodity persists. The demand curve overtime will be inflected curve. Over time, the monopoly firm finds it useful to adopt newer technologies (AC1 becomes AC2) so that prices would reduce (OP1 becoming OP2) and it creates the illusion of an 'up-to-date' good being sold by the monopoly firm. Supernormal profits will persist in the long run due to TINA factor continuation.

7. Monopolistic Competition

7.1 Subjective (dd) and Objective (DD) Demand Curves

Chamberlin's Analysis: Monopolistic competition = Brand vs Brand competition
Curves: dd (individual firm's aspirations) | DD (group behavior pattern)
1 D2 d1 d2 R1 R2 R3 R4
dd (Subjective Demand Curve): Trendline depicting commercial aspirations of that firm. Gently sloped downwards - a fall in price would hopefully lead to significant increase in demand. When dd shifts downwards, the firm is lowering price.
DD (Objective Demand Curve): Captures behavior pattern of all firms involved. Steeply sloped downwards since the Law of Averages would force the increment in market to be much lesser than aspired. When DD shifts to the right, firms are exiting the market. When DD shifts left, new firms are entering.

7.2 Short-Run Supernormal Profit in Monopolistic Competition

Short Run Analysis: In the short run, firms can earn supernormal profits. Due to entry/exit dynamics (Schumpeterian dynamics), this leads to interdependence among existing firms, giving rise to entry/exit of firms affecting group dynamics. Firms indulge in product differentiation (cosmetically - aroma, color, size) serving the same purpose. Different firms have different objectives (profit max, sales max, welfare) requiring different pricing strategies.

8. Oligopoly

8.1 Dominant Firm Price Leadership Model

Structure: Few large firms (dominant) + Many small firms
Market Division: Shared market between dominant firm and smaller firms

Shared Market (Smaller Firms)

d,Qs Price SF D Es P P-Es S1 S2

Dominant Firm

1 Q OP DF price = OP1 < smaller firms' price OP
Model Explanation: In Figure 1, Demand curve (D) shows demand that consumers and all firms (smaller and dominant) would like to cater to. The Pn-SF curve represents supply function of smaller firms. Es becomes the equilibrium from which smaller firms operate. The average price charged by smaller firms is OP and supply size is P-Es units.

Once the dominant firm realizes that smaller firms on average would be charging price OP, it would begin operating from point A in Figure 2. Hence, the financial functions (AR, MR, AC, MC) will be based on position of point A. Since the DF is the dominant firm, it can opt for profit maximization and produce OQ units at price OP1 (NOTE: OP1 < OP).

The dominant firm passes on its low price into the market. Since it lowered the price, demand increases from P-Es to P1D1. The market share of smaller firms is P1S1 and DF's market share is S1D1. In order to increase its market share further, the DF would continue the price cutting strategy by even sacrificing maximum profits.

Traits of Dominant Firm: (1) Deep pocketed - benefit of cross financialization to tide over sub-normal profits. (2) Able to create entry barriers like IPR (Trademarks, etc.), constant tech migration, large scale promotions. (3) Able to sustain the feeding of a large market size (30+%) individually. (4) Can create cartels to become price leaders.
